# Navier–Stokes: Natural-Language Proof vs Lean

**v0.1 - preliminary, unreviewed**

An attempt to audit, at scale, where OpenAI's Navier–Stokes paper and its Lean formalization differ.

A tool that audits differences between natural-language and Lean proofs, run against a set of curated targets in the OpenAI Navier-Stokes paper.

## Disclaimer

I am a software engineer, not a mathematician.

With the exception of this disclaimer, and the background section, the rest of the documentation is mostly AI-generated.

The results of this tool are generated by an LLM judge and should be taken with a grain of salt - the decoy test suggested at least 1 in 10 matches could point at the wrong Lean theorem. Treat them as breadcrumbs that speed up independent auditing.

## Background

In 2026 OpenAI published a paper, *Finite time blowup for Navier–Stokes* ([PDF](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf)), together with a formalization in Lean 4 ([openai/NavierStokesAndEuler](https://github.com/openai/NavierStokesAndEuler)).

Although the Lean proof compiles and its main theorems pass their checks, the Lean sometimes drifts from what the natural language paper says. Bastounis, Circelli and Hansen compared some of the results by hand and found places where they differ ([arXiv:2610.08144](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08144)).

This project attempts to audit those differences at scale, using AI to build metadata about each statement: which Lean theorem matches each result in the paper, what each side assumes and proves, and where the two differ.

The detailed comparison covered all of Section 8, Lemma 10.5, and the numbered steps inside three proofs. Pointed at Section 8 and the proof of Lemma 10.5, where Bastounis et al. found their two discrepancies, it reported both without being told what to look for.

The tool also flagged eq. (8.10), in the proof of Lemma 8.2: the paper needs p+3 derivatives, the general Lean lemma that formalizes it needs l+4. Reading the source afterwards, the Lean proves its Lemma 8.6 by applying that lemma with l = 1, which is exactly where its m+5 comes from. The paper proves Lemma 8.6 separately, without (8.10), so the one extra derivative traces back to a single choice in the Lean. Whether this matters is not something I can meaningfully comment on.

## What the tool does

In plain terms, for each numbered result in the paper:

1. **Find its counterpart in the Lean.** A Lean file is a list of *declarations*: named theorems and definitions. A language model reads through them and names the one that states the paper's result. It does this three times, independently (three *samples*), and a match only counts if all three pick the same declaration. If they don't agree, it searches again in files it hasn't looked at yet, up to three rounds.
2. **Compare the two statements.** For each matched pair, the model writes down what each side assumes and what it concludes, and lists every difference it sees, sorted into types such as "the paper claims a stronger bound than the Lean proves" or "the Lean assumes something the paper doesn't state". Again three samples.
3. **Check the quotes.** Every difference has to quote the paper (with page) and the Lean (with file and line). Ordinary code, not the model, checks that each quote really is at the place cited.
4. **Report.** The three samples are combined without voting. A difference reported by only one sample stays in the tables, marked 1 of 3, so you can see how consistent the model was.

There are also a few checks that need no model: a scan of the Lean source for `sorry` and other shortcuts, a search for theorems that are copies of each other, and a count of how many layers of definitions sit under each statement.

The tool doesn't say whether the main theorem is true; that's the Lean proof's job. It doesn't say whether a difference matters either. Many are harmless rewordings, and some might mean the paper claims more than the Lean proves.

## What it found

**The map.** 77 of the paper's 80 numbered results got a Lean match that all 3 samples agreed on. The other 3 got 2 out of 3, and none came up empty. There's no blueprint for this formalization (a blueprint is the usual document linking a paper's results to their Lean versions), and the paper references in the Lean comments use an older draft's numbering, so I don't know of another map like this. A decoy test suggests at least 1 in 10 of the confident matches could be wrong.

**The differences.** These were only extracted for a pilot: Section 8, Lemma 10.5, and the numbered equations inside the proofs of Lemmas 8.2, 8.6 and 10.5. That's 58 paper/Lean pairs and 1,014 differences, 322 of which all 3 samples reported. Most are noise: different names, or the Lean spelling out conditions the paper leaves implicit. Only 17 of the 58 pairs are on the path to the main theorems that the tool could detect.

**Validation.** Both discrepancies from Bastounis et al. showed up in all 3 samples: Lemma 8.6 (m+4 derivatives in the paper, m+5 in the Lean) and eq. (10.19) in the proof of Lemma 10.5 (the first term of the pressure-flux bound has a different form). That's weaker evidence than it sounds. I put Lemmas 8.6 and 10.5 on the target list because of their paper. The prompts never mention the discrepancies, but the tool didn't pick where to look. And the Lean docstring for the Lemma 8.6 theorem says "five torus derivatives lost", so the Lean was never hiding it.

**Eq. (8.10)**, the first shortlist entry. The paper needs p+3 derivatives there; the Lean version, a general multiplier lemma, needs l+4. I followed this one up by hand after the run, with Claude's help; a mathematician still needs to look at it. The Lean gets its Lemma 8.6 bound by plugging l = 1 into that general lemma, which is where the m+5 comes from. The paper proves Lemma 8.6 on its own and never uses (8.10). So in the Lean the two differences have one cause, and in the paper they're unrelated. The Lean lemma also differs from (8.10) in a few other ways. Details are in [METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md#810-followed-up-by-hand).

**Static checks** (source text only, nothing compiled). There's no `sorry`, `axiom`, `admit`, `native_decide` or `unsafe` anywhere under `NavierStokes/`. The only `sorry`s in the repo are the intended placeholders in the Comparator challenge files. That matches what the repo says about itself, though only Lean can confirm which axioms a proof actually uses. 474 theorems turn out to be copies of each other, in 187 groups, mostly the same small fact proved again in another file; one of them is restated 83 times. Each matched result also gets a definition depth: how many layers of the formalization's own definitions sit under its statement before you reach Mathlib, Lean's standard library. It ranges from 0 to 55, and the deep ones are where a human read is most worth having. Details are in [METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md#static-checks-on-the-lean-source).

## Known weaknesses

The biggest one is that matches are made on resemblance. A Lean declaration gets matched to a paper result because its statement looks like the paper's, not because the proof uses it where the paper uses that result. (8.10) shows why that's a problem: the declaration all 3 samples picked isn't on the path to the main theorems that the tool could detect, and the one the proof does use got 2 votes out of 3.

There's also no dependency graph on the paper side, so the tool can't see when the paper and the Lean take different routes to the same result. The link between (8.10) and Lemma 8.6 came from reading the code, not from the tool.

Finally, the Lean index comes from parsing the `.lean` files rather than from Lean itself. The static checks found one way it goes wrong: 3.4% of theorem statements were cut off partway, and while it was looking for matches the model saw 3.3% of the statements it read without their full conclusion. The comparison step wasn't affected. Checking against Lean itself would need a build, which I haven't done.

A version 2 would fix all three: build the Lean index and dependency graph from Lean, build the matching graph for the paper, match on structure as well as resemblance, and produce a machine-inferred blueprint that people can correct. There's a sketch in [METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md#planned-fixes).
