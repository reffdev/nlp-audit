# Navier–Stokes: Natural-Language Proof vs Lean

> **v0.2 (October 2026).** v0.1 has known defects, found by an adversarial review: paper text was cut short in some prompts, the paper-quote check ignored LaTeX commands, and "reported by all 3 samples" counted overlapping citations rather than checked agreement. v0.2 is a corrected rerun and re-scoring with the same model and method, not a new method; the corrections reduced every confirmed number. Before and after figures are in [CHANGES-v0.2.md](CHANGES-v0.2.md). A v2 is in design and will supersede both.

**v0.2 - preliminary, unreviewed**

An attempt to audit, at scale, where OpenAI's Navier–Stokes paper and its Lean formalization differ.

A tool that audits differences between natural-language and Lean proofs, run against a set of curated targets in the OpenAI Navier–Stokes paper.

## Disclaimer

I am a software engineer, not a mathematician.

With the exception of this disclaimer, and the 'Background' section, the rest of the documentation is drafted by AI and reviewed and edited by me.

The results of this tool are generated by an LLM judge and should be taken with a grain of salt - 0 of 21 decoys got a confident false match in v0.2 (2 of 21 in v0.1); the test is too small to distinguish these and does not bound the error rate on real matches. Treat them as breadcrumbs that speed up independent auditing.

## Background

In 2026 OpenAI published a paper, *Finite time blowup for Navier–Stokes* ([PDF](https://cdn.openai.com/pdf/32d9f210-8b73-45e0-91bc-82a30aef8a9a/navier-stokes.pdf)), together with a formalization in Lean 4 ([openai/NavierStokesAndEuler](https://github.com/openai/NavierStokesAndEuler)).

Although the Lean proof compiles and its main theorems pass their checks, the Lean sometimes drifts from what the natural language paper says. Bastounis, Circelli and Hansen compared some of the results by hand and found places where they differ ([arXiv:2610.08144](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.08144)).

This project attempts to audit those differences at scale, using AI to build metadata about each statement: which Lean theorem matches each result in the paper, what each side assumes and proves, and where the two differ.

The detailed comparison covered all of Section 8, Lemma 10.5, and the numbered steps inside three proofs. Pointed at Section 8 and the proof of Lemma 10.5, where Bastounis et al. found their two discrepancies, it reproduced Lemma 8.6 robustly; under the corrected method it did not confirm (10.19).

The tool also surfaced, weakly, a candidate at eq. (8.10), in the proof of Lemma 8.2: the paper assumes p+3 derivatives, the general Lean lemma that formalizes it assumes l+4. The link to Lemma 8.6 came from reading the source and was confirmed by an outside review: the Lean proves its Lemma 8.6 by applying that lemma with l = 1, which is where its m+5 comes from, while the paper proves Lemma 8.6 separately, without (8.10). Bastounis, Circelli and Hansen's Remark 3.2 had already traced the mechanism. Whether this matters is not something I can meaningfully comment on.

## What the tool does

In plain terms, for each numbered result in the paper:

1. **Find its counterpart in the Lean.** A Lean file is a list of *declarations*: named theorems and definitions. A language model reads through them and names the one that states the paper's result. It does this three times, independently (three *samples*), and a match only counts if all three pick the same declaration. If they don't agree, it searches again in files it hasn't looked at yet, up to three rounds.
2. **Compare the two statements.** For each matched pair, the model writes down what each side assumes and what it concludes, and lists every difference it sees, sorted into types such as "the paper claims a stronger bound than the Lean proves" or "the Lean assumes something the paper doesn't state". Again three samples.
3. **Check the quotes.** Every difference has to quote the paper (with page) and the Lean (with file and line). Ordinary code, not the model, checks each quote: a Lean quote must be at the cited lines, and a paper quote is checked on the cited page and in the text the model was shown, as exact, normalized, or unresolved.
4. **Group and adjudicate.** The three samples are combined without voting: differences that cite overlapping Lean lines are grouped, and three further samples then answer only whether each group's descriptions say the same thing. A difference counts as agreed only when they all say yes. A difference reported by only one sample stays in the tables, marked 1 of 3, so you can see how consistent the model was.

There are also a few checks that need no model: a scan of the Lean source for `sorry` and other shortcuts, a search for theorems that are copies of each other, and a count of how many layers of definitions sit under each statement.

The tool doesn't say whether the main theorem is true; that's the Lean proof's job. It doesn't say whether a difference matters either. Many are harmless rewordings, and some might mean the paper claims more than the Lean proves.

## What it found

**One of two published discrepancies reproduced robustly.** Both were put on the target list because Bastounis et al. had found them, so this is recovery, not discovery; the prompts never describe them. Lemma 8.6 (m+4 derivatives in the paper, m+5 in the Lean): all 3 samples reported it at the Lean line where it sits, and the same check fires on 0 of the other 66 pairs compared. (The Lean docstring for that theorem says "five torus derivatives lost", so the Lean never hid it.) Eq. (10.19), in the proof of Lemma 10.5, is not confirmed. A location-and-type check counts 3 of 3 samples, but that check also fires on 60 of the other 66 pairs, and the difference "the first part of the bound differs" has citations from only 2 samples, confirmed by 2 of 3 adjudication samples. Reading the three samples, the third described a different observation: the extra 1/R² summand inside the first factor, filed as a stronger bound at a neighbouring line.

**The map.** 77 of the paper's 80 numbered results got a Lean match all 3 samples agreed on, in both v0.1 and v0.2, and none came up empty; which 3 are weak changed. But the matches themselves moved: 59 of the 88 targets (results and proof steps) changed their top declaration or their set of agreed declarations between the two runs. In 12 the top declaration stayed and only the other agreed declarations changed, in 13 the old top is still among the agreed declarations, in 14 the two runs' agreed sets overlap, and in 20 they share nothing. By distance, none moved within a cluster of duplicate statements, 6 moved within the same file, and 2 to a different folder; the other 39 stayed in the same folder, which says little because almost all declarations are in one folder. Prompts also changed between the runs, so this is sensitivity to changed inputs, not a measurement of sampling noise; that measurement is deferred to v2. There's no blueprint for this formalization (a blueprint is the usual document linking a paper's results to their Lean versions), and the paper references in the Lean comments use an older draft's numbering, so I don't know of another map like this; but given the above, treat any single match as a candidate. In the decoy test, 0 of 21 decoys received a confident false match in v0.2 (2 of 21 in v0.1); 21 items is too few to distinguish these.

**Agreement.** 1,712 grouped differences; 442 with overlapping citations from all three samples; 206 where three adjudication samples agree they describe the same difference. The v0.1 docs reported 422 (322 at the v0.1 tag) under the weaker criterion of overlapping citations alone.

**The differences.** These were only extracted for a pilot: Section 8, Lemma 10.5, and the numbered equations inside the proofs of Lemmas 8.2, 8.6 and 10.5. That's 67 paper/Lean pairs (43 for 9 results, 24 for 8 proof steps). Most differences are noise: different names, or the Lean spelling out conditions the paper leaves implicit. Only 22 of the 67 pairs are on the path to the main theorems that the tool could detect.

**Eq. (8.10).** In the proof of Lemma 8.2, the paper's (8.10) assumes p+3 derivatives; the general Lean lemma `applyMultiplier_finiteJets` assumes l+4. In v0.1 the tool produced that lemma as a 2-of-3 candidate for (8.10), with the difference reported by all 3 samples; v0.2 reproduced the candidate at 2 of 3 and the difference at 1 of 3. The rest came from reading the source, with Claude's help, and was confirmed by the adversarial review: the Lean proves its Lemma 8.6 bound by applying that lemma with l = 1, which is where its m+5 comes from, whereas the paper derives (8.19) and (8.10) separately (by the same Diophantine and Fourier-summability mechanism) and does not cite (8.10) in the proof of Lemma 8.6. Bastounis, Circelli and Hansen's Remark 3.2 already traced the derivative loss to the series in `coefficient_seminorm_bound`; what is added here is only the link to (8.10) through the l = 1 case. The Lean lemma accepts any polynomially bounded multiplier and has no zero-mode condition. Σ(1+|k|)⁻³ over ℤ² converges, so summability is not the open question; what nobody has done is a checked sharper coefficient estimate propagated through the Lean lemmas. Details are in [METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md#810-followed-up-by-hand).

**Static checks** (source text only, nothing compiled). There's no `sorry`, `axiom`, `admit`, `native_decide` or `unsafe` anywhere under `NavierStokes/`. The only `sorry`s in the repo are the intended placeholders in the Comparator challenge files. That matches what the repo says about itself, though only Lean can confirm which axioms a proof actually uses. 474 declarations fall into 187 normalized-statement clusters (287 beyond one representative each); the largest clusters are trivial inequalities, and the normalization ignores universe levels and section hypotheses. Each matched result also gets a definition depth: the longest chain of the formalization's own definitions under its statement before you reach Mathlib, the main mathematics library for Lean, in the source-resolved graph. It ranges from 0 to 55; it makes no claim about review effort. Details are in [METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md#static-checks-on-the-lean-source).

## Known weaknesses

**Matches aren't stable between runs.** Between v0.1 and v0.2, 59 of 88 targets changed their top declaration or their agreed set; in 20 the two runs' agreed sets share nothing (the full split is under What it found and in CHANGES-v0.2.md). 59 of 88 targets changed match between runs; 20 share no agreed declaration with v0.1. Prompts also changed, so this is sensitivity to changed inputs, not a measurement of sampling noise. That measurement is deferred to v2.

**Overlapping citations are the same observation only about half the time.** Of the 442 groups where all 3 samples cite overlapping Lean lines, 206 were confirmed by adjudication as describing the same difference. Across the 896 adjudicated groups with 2 or more samples, all 3 adjudication samples said "same" for 474 and none did for 371.

The biggest structural one is that matches are made on resemblance. A Lean declaration gets matched to a paper result because its statement looks like the paper's, not because the proof uses it where the paper uses that result. (8.10) shows why that's a problem: in both runs the declarations all 3 samples picked include ones that aren't on the path to the main theorems that the tool could detect, while the one the Lean proof uses got 2 of 3.

There's also no dependency graph on the paper side, so the tool can't see when the paper and the Lean take different routes to the same result. The link between (8.10) and Lemma 8.6 came from reading the code, not from the tool.

Finally, the Lean index comes from parsing the `.lean` files rather than from Lean itself. The static checks found one way it goes wrong: 3.4% of theorem statements were cut off partway, and while it was looking for matches the model saw 3.3% of the statements it read without their full conclusion. The comparison step wasn't affected. Checking against Lean itself would need a build, which I haven't done.

A version 2 would fix all three: build the Lean index and dependency graph from Lean, build the matching graph for the paper, match on structure as well as resemblance, and produce a machine-inferred blueprint that people can correct. There's a sketch in [METHODOLOGY.md](METHODOLOGY.md#planned-fixes).
